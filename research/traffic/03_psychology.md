# 3. Why people break traffic laws, and how that connects to other offending

## One trait behind risky driving and crime (measured)

- **Junger, West & Timman (2001), *Journal of Research in Crime and Delinquency*.**
  - **Data:** 1,531 people in police-registered crashes, matched to the national offending
    register (no self-report).
  - **Method:** controlling for age, sex and exposure, people whose risky behaviour caused or
    contributed to their crash were compared with those whose behaviour didn't.
  - **Results:** the risky drivers had odds ratios of **2.6 for a police record of violent
    crime, 2.5 for vandalism, 1.5 for property crime and 5.3 for traffic crime**.
  - **Authors' reading:** a common factor, "a general disregard for the long term adverse
    consequences of one's actions … risk-taking, impulsiveness, or lack of self-control".
- **Watson, Watson & Siskind (2015), *Accident Analysis & Prevention*.**
  - **Data:** 84,456 speeding offences in Queensland.
  - **Results:** repeat high-range speeders were more often male, young and on provisional
    licences, with more prior traffic offences and crashes, and **55.2% had a criminal
    history**. They were more likely than once-only low-range speeders to have drug offences
    and offences against order.
- **Rose (2000), UK Home Office Research Study 206, *The criminal histories of serious traffic
  offenders*:** serious traffic offenders have substantial mainstream criminal histories. The
  UK "offender targeting" strategy rests on this.
- **Retting, Ulmer & Williams (1999):** red-light runners in crashes were more often young
  men, with prior moving violations, drunk-driving convictions and invalid licences.
- **NHTSA, speeding 2022:** speeding drivers in fatal crashes were about twice as likely as
  others to be drunk (38% vs 18%) or unlicensed (29% vs 15%).
- **The theory:** Gottfredson & Hirschi's general theory of crime (1990). Low self-control
  (impulsive, short-sighted, risk-seeking, physical, self-centred, quick-tempered) shows up
  across settings: crime, reckless driving, drinking, smoking, accidents.
  - **Keane, Maxim & Teevan (1993), *J. Research in Crime and Delinquency*:** tested it on
    drink-driving and supported it.

## The psychological factors (measured)

| Factor | Evidence | In the model |
|---|---|---|
| **Sensation seeking** | **Jonah (1997), *Accident Analysis & Prevention*:** a review of 40 studies; sensation seeking correlates with risky driving at **r = 0.30–0.40**. **Zuckerman; Ball, Farnill & Wangeman (1984):** higher in men, declining steeply with age. | Higher for young men; drives speeding, overtaking and close following. |
| **Driving anger** | **Deffenbacher, Oetting & Lynch (1994):** Driving Anger Scale with six facets (hostile gestures, illegal driving, police presence, slow driving, discourtesy, obstructions); **men angered more by police presence and slow driving, women by illegal driving and obstructions**. **Dahlen et al. (2005):** sensation seeking, impulsiveness and boredom proneness add to anger in predicting aggressive and risky driving. | Rises with neuroticism and with low agreeableness; drives tailgating, aggressive overtaking and passing on the right. |
| **General aggression** | **Lajunen & Parker (2001), *AA&P*:** verbal aggression works on driving through driver anger, physical aggression directly. Among men, anger and aggression decline with age. | Folded into anger and the deviance factor. |
| **Big Five** | **Dahlen & White (2006), *Personality and Individual Differences*:** the Big Five, sensation seeking and anger together predict unsafe driving. Low agreeableness and low conscientiousness, plus high neuroticism, recur across studies. | Self-control is built mainly from conscientiousness, plus agreeableness and (inversely) neuroticism. |
| **Violations predict crashes** | **de Winter & Dodou (2010), *J. Safety Research*:** meta-analysis of the Driver Behaviour Questionnaire; its **violations** factor (deliberate rule-breaking, distinct from errors and lapses) predicts crash involvement. af Wåhlberg et al. (2015) re-analysis: weaker once common-method bias is controlled. | Violations are one propensity, separate from skill. |
| **Age and sex** | **Yagil (1998), *Transportation Research F*:** young drivers and men hold more permissive attitudes to traffic law. NHTSA survey: half of 16–20-year-olds are speeders; men 36% vs women 28%. | Age and sex shape sensation seeking and self-control, so they carry through to every violation. |
| **Norms: "keep up with traffic"** | NHTSA 2011: 64% of speeders strongly agree that people should keep up with the flow of traffic, against 42% of nonspeeders. | A driver's desired speed leans toward the speed of the traffic around it. |
| **Deterrence** | NHTSA 2011: ticketed drivers believe they can go 10.6 mph over before being ticketed. Feest (1968): compliance depends on being seen. | Violations are more likely with nobody near (a hook for future police presence). |

## What this means for the generator

One latent **deviance** factor, made mostly of low self-control plus sensation seeking and
anger, drives two things:
- **Traffic violations:** speeding, rolling stops, passing on the right, tailgating, red lights,
  failing to yield to pedestrians.
- **Wider offending:** a criminal record whose odds follow Junger's ratios, against the 8%
  felony base rate.

The same people who roll stop signs are, on average, a little more likely to have other
offences, and much more likely to have traffic ones. The link is a tendency, not a rule. Most
speeders have no record, and the 4511 violations are minor misdemeanours.
