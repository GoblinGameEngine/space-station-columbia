# Road surface markings

## United States (MUTCD Part 3)
- **Yellow** separates opposing traffic (since 1971). **White** separates same-direction lanes and marks the right edge.
- Broken lines are **10 ft (3 m) segments with 30 ft (9 m) gaps**, or the same ratio.
- Lines are **4–6 in (100–150 mm)** wide. Wide (6 in) edge lines reduce run-off-road crashes.
- **Double solid yellow**: no passing either way. Solid + broken: passing only from the broken side.
- Edge lines may be left out where curbs, parking or bike lanes delineate the edge (urban streets).
- **Stop lines** are white, 12–24 in wide, about 4 ft (1.2 m) before the crosswalk. Crosswalks are two transverse lines, or "zebra" (continental) bars on busy streets.
- Raised pavement markers: Botts' dots and retroreflective markers (white facing traffic, red on the reverse side).

## Canada (TAC MUTCDC)
- Yellow and white are used as in the US. A single solid yellow forbids crossing.
- **Continuity lines** (short, closely spaced dashes) mark lane drops and exits.
- Cat's eyes (studs) are used in some areas.

## United Kingdom (TSRGD, Traffic Signs Manual ch. 5)
- **All longitudinal lines are white.** Yellow is kerbside parking control only: double yellow = no waiting at any time.
- Centre lines are **4 m mark / 2 m gap at 40 mph or less**, and **6 m / 3 m above 40 mph**. They are 100 or 150 mm wide.
- A **hazard warning line** is the inverse (2 m / 4 m or 3 m / 6 m): gaps shorter than the marks mean a hazard ahead.
- Double white lines: solid on your side means don't cross.
- Edge lines are solid white. Give way markings are double broken lines; stop is a solid line with "STOP".
- **Studs:** white between lanes, red on the left edge, amber by the central reserve, green at slip roads and lay-bys.

## Continental Europe (Vienna Convention)
- Longitudinal markings are white. Norway uses yellow between opposing directions; Finland uses yellow for no-overtaking.
- Yellow is for temporary works and parking bans (solid = no stopping, broken = no parking).
- **Zebra crossings** (wide white bars along the traffic direction) are universal.
- Towns often leave edge lines off kerbed streets.
