# Railway level crossings

## United States (MUTCD Part 8)
- **Every** public crossing has a **crossbuck** (white X with black "RAILROAD CROSSING") on the right of each approach, with a "number of tracks" plaque if there are two or more tracks.
- **Advance warning:** a round yellow sign with a black X and "RR" on every approach, plus an "RXR" pavement marking and stop line on paved roads.
- **Passive crossings** have the crossbuck with a YIELD or STOP sign, and no lights.
- **Active crossings** have flashing red light pairs and a bell; **gates** are added on busier or faster roads. Gates always come with flashing lights.
- The choice depends on train speed and frequency, road traffic, crash history and sight distance.

## Canada (Grade Crossings Regulations 2014, Transport Canada standards)
- The crossing sign is a white X with a red border. The same passive-to-active ladder applies, set by railway design speed, road and pedestrian use, and the number of tracks.
- Gate arms carry 3 flashing lights, lower in 10–15 s and rise in 6–12 s.

## United Kingdom
- **Automatic half barrier (AHBC):**
  - a steady amber for about 3 s, then twin flashing reds (wig-wags);
  - an audible alarm;
  - half-width barriers on each side, so no one is trapped.
- **Full barrier:** manned or CCTV-controlled only. There are no automatic full barriers, because they could trap vehicles.
- **Open crossing:** wig-wags and a St Andrew's cross, no barriers. Used on quiet roads.
- **User-worked:** gates or barriers and miniature red and green lights, on private and farm roads.
- **Countdown markers** (3-2-1 bars) and advance warning triangles on the approaches.

## Continental Europe
- A red-and-white **St Andrew's cross** (Andreaskreuz) at the crossing.
- **Countdown beacons** divide the distance from the advance warning sign to the stop line into three.
- Flashing reds (a white or yellow flashing "clear" aspect in some countries) and **half barriers**, which are most common on busy roads.
- Rural and low-traffic crossings often have just the cross, sometimes a STOP sign.
- France adds "un train peut en cacher un autre" (one train can hide another) at multi-track crossings.

## The common rule
Protection rises with exposure (road traffic, train speed and frequency, how built-up the area is, sight lines):
1. **Passive:** cross and stop/yield (rural, farm and quiet lanes).
2. **Lights and bell** (moderate traffic).
3. **Lights, bell and half barriers** (busy roads, towns, faster trains).
4. **Full barriers, supervised** (town centres, high speed).
