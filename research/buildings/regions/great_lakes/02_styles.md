# Why buildings look the way they do: scholarship (2026-10-05)

A generator needs to know *why* a form exists, so it combines parts the way real builders did and not at random.
The literature gives four kinds of answer:

1. **Folk tradition**: plans carried by migrants.
2. **Fashion**: styles spread by pattern books and catalogues.
3. **Economics and technology**: lot, frame, materials, codes.
4. **Commercial communication**: the building as a sign.

## 1. Form before style: the "shape first" rule

- **McAlester, *A Field Guide to American Houses*** (1984; rev. 2013): the standard identification guide. Its key
  point for us is that **a house is a form plus a style applied to it**.
  - The form is the shape: roof type, massing, the number of storeys, the plan.
  - The style is the ornament: details of porch, window, cornice and door.
  - The guide identifies by form first (its Pictorial Key starts with the roof), and the same few forms carry many
    styles.
  - **For us: the token hierarchy should be form, then style**, just as the vehicle fleet separates the loft (form)
    from the dressing (style).
- **Folk forms (Kniffen, "Folk Housing: Key to Diffusion", 1965)**: house *plans* travel with migrants along routes
  and persist for generations.
  - The **I-house** (two storeys, one room deep, two rooms wide, gable ends, usually a rear ell) was named by Kniffen
    in 1936 after Indiana, Illinois and Iowa. It is the farm house of the Corn Belt.
  - The Great Lakes add the **upright-and-wing** (a gable-front block with a side wing, Greek Revival roots, carried
    from New England and New York into Michigan, Ohio and Wisconsin), the **gable-front** house, and in Ontario the
    **centre-gable Ontario cottage**: 1½ storeys, symmetrical, a steep centre gable often with a pointed or round
    window, and a full verandah.
  - **For us: rural and early town bands draw from folk forms**, and the settlement's founding *migration stream*
    decides which ones.
- **Glassie, *Folk Housing in Middle Virginia*** (1975): treats houses as generated by a *grammar* of rules
  (symmetry, room units, additions). It is the humanities precedent for our shape grammars (section 4).

## 2. Fashion: how styles spread

- **Pattern books, then catalogue houses.**
  - **Pattern books** (Downing, 1840s-50s) spread the Gothic and Italianate.
  - **Mail-order kits** followed. Sears sold about 70-75,000 houses in 1908-40; Aladdin (Bay City, Michigan) and
    Montgomery Ward sold more. They shipped by rail with pre-cut parts.
  - **For us**: identical models recur across towns, mirrored and recoloured. *Repetition with variation is
    realistic*, not a shortcut.
- **Gowans, *The Comfortable House*** (1986): more houses were built in 1890-1930 than in all earlier US history. He
  classifies them (the foursquare, bungalow, homestead temple, cottage and revivals) and traces each to catalogue
  sources.
  - These two types dominate the Great Lakes town band: the **foursquare** (a 2-storey box, hip roof, a dormer and a
    full porch) and the **bungalow**.
- **Postwar**: FHA standards and builders' plan services standardised houses again. Minimal Traditional, then the
  ranch, split-level and raised ranch (05 of urban_layout; 01 here, the FHA).
- **The time lag**: a style reached small towns 5-15 years after the cities, and lingered. Rural Michigan was building
  Greek Revival upright-and-wings in the 1880s.
  - **For us**: a band's style mix is its era *shifted later and smeared* by the town's size and remoteness.

## 3. Commercial buildings: composition types (Longstreth)

- **Longstreth, *The Buildings of Main Street*** (1987; 2000) classifies commercial buildings by **facade
  composition**, not style. That is exactly the structural vocabulary a facade generator needs:
  - **One-part commercial block**: one storey, a storefront plus a sign band or parapet.
  - **Two-part commercial block**: the most common type, 2-4 storeys. The *lower zone* is public (storefronts) and
    the *upper zone* private (offices, flats, a lodge hall), with a clear division (a belt course or the storefront
    cornice).
  - **Two-part vertical block, three-part vertical block**: taller, with base, shaft and capital.
  - **Enframed window wall, central block with wings, temple front, vault, arcaded block, stacked vertical block**.
  - **Enframed window wall** (often 20th century): little or no division between floors, large glass, modest and
    abstract decoration.
- **For us**: Longstreth's types *are* our commercial grammar's top-level rules. The style (Italianate, Romanesque,
  Commercial, Deco, Modern) is dressing applied to a type, as McAlester does for houses.
  - The catalog's existing store traits (`facade`, `cornice`, `upper_use`) map onto the two-part block.

Sources: [Longstreth, Buildings of Main Street (CCA)](https://www.cca.qc.ca/en/search/details/library/publication/44510458);
[Texas Historical Commission: commercial architecture](https://www.thc.texas.gov/public/upload/preserve/survey/survey/Commercial%20Architecture.pdf);
[Naperville downtown survey forms (Longstreth types applied)](https://www.naperville.il.us/contentassets/94290172fa65443896b63e35af2deab9/cmp-dpa-downtown-architectural-survey-forms.pdf);

- **Venturi, Scott Brown and Izenour, *Learning from Las Vegas*** (1972): on the car strip the **sign is the
  architecture**.
  - The **duck** is a building whose form is its symbol.
  - The **decorated shed** is a plain box with a sign in front, set behind a car park and read from a moving car.
  - **For us**: strip-band commercial is a *decorated shed*. Most of the variety goes into the pylon sign, the fascia
    and the canopy, and the box stays plain. Occasional *ducks* (a giant donut, a coffee-pot diner) are period
    landmarks (Lynch, urban_layout 02).

Sources: [Learning from Las Vegas (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Learning_from_Las_Vegas);

## 4. Generating style: shape grammars and procedural buildings

- **Stiny and Mitchell, "The Palladian Grammar"** (1978): a parametric shape grammar that generates Palladio's villa
  plans. It showed that a *style is a set of rules*, and that new, convincing members of the style come from those
  rules.
- **Koning and Eizenberg, "The language of the prairie: Frank Lloyd Wright's prairie houses"** (1981): a grammar for
  Wright's Prairie houses.
  - It starts from the **fireplace core** and adds Froebel-block masses around it recursively.
  - The grammar generated new, believable Prairie houses.
  - It is set in our region (the Chicago suburbs) and our style list (`prairie_box`).
- **Müller, Wonka, Haegler, Ulmer, Van Gool, "Procedural Modeling of Buildings"** (SIGGRAPH 2006; CGA shape):
  - **Mass model first**: volumetric shapes, unions and roofs.
  - **Facade next**: split rules (divide a face into floors, then bays) and repeat rules (fill a floor with window
    tiles).
  - **Detail last**: windows, doors and ornament.
  - It is the method behind CityEngine and the direct template for our facade tokens.
  - Its successor, Schwarz and Müller's *Advanced procedural modeling of architecture* (2015), adds queries between
    shapes, such as "is this wall facing a neighbour?". That is the code rule in 01 §2.
- **For us**: the vehicle fleet already works this way (loft, then panels, then dressing). Buildings get the same
  three stages:
  1. **Mass model**: footprint, storeys, roof.
  2. **Facade split**: floors, bays, openings, by the composition type (Longstreth) or the house form (McAlester).
  3. **Dressing**: the style tokens.

Sources: [Stiny and Mitchell 1978 (CMU copy)](https://www.andrew.cmu.edu/user/ramesh/teaching/course/48-747/subFrames/readings/Stiny&MItchell-1978-EPB5_5-18.ThePalladianGrammar.pdf);

## 5. Why some buildings please: environmental psychology

- **Prospect and refuge** (Appleton 1975; Hildebrand, *Origins of Architectural Pleasure*, 1999): we like places that
  offer a protected vantage (refuge) with a view out (prospect).
  - Hildebrand adds *enticement* (partial views that invite exploration) and *order with complexity*.
  - In buildings this means deep porches, bay windows, low eaves over a sheltered entry, window seats, and Wright's
    low ceilings opening to bright rooms.
  - **For us**: the porch, the bay and the inglenook are not decoration. They score high, which is why they recur
    across styles.
- **Facade complexity** (Stamps; Nasar):
  - Stamps found three factors (surface complexity, silhouette complexity and articulation). **Surface complexity
    weighs most** (β ≈ .72).
  - Preference rises with complexity up to a point, *if the complexity is organised* into groups (bays, repeated
    windows). Unorganised complexity scores low.
  - Nasar's *Evaluative Image of the City* (1998) found that people across groups agree on the qualities that
    please: **naturalness, upkeep, openness, historical significance, order**. They also agree on what displeases:
    dilapidation, disorder, nuisances (signs, wires, traffic).
  - **For us**: condition (upkeep) and organised repetition drive how pleasant a generated street feels; so do the
    clutter of the strip and the order of the old block. This links straight to district condition (urban_layout 03).
