# 2. Roles in contemporary suburban and urban middle America

## 2.1 The classic ethnographies
- **Middletown** (Lynd & Lynd 1929; Muncie, Indiana; restudied as Middletown III, 1977–82).
  - A small city divided into a **business class** that "worked with people and symbols" and a
    **working class** that "worked with things": "as different as two different tribes", with
    different values that did not mingle. The working class was laid off without notice; the
    business class almost never.
  - Middletown III (Caplow et al.) surveyed family roles, kinship, neighbourhoods, religion and
    women's work, and found the composite family "in splendid condition"
    ([Lynds](https://www.newworldencyclopedia.org/entry/Robert_and_Helen_Lynd); [ICPSR Middletown III–IV](https://www.icpsr.umich.edu/web/ICPSR/studies/4604)).
  - **For us:** `RL.class` ∈ {business, working}. It is a homophily dimension (ties form within
    class) and an axis of story (Polti 24: the superior and inferior rival).
- **The Levittowners** (Gans 1967).
  - New homeowners in a brand-new suburb built their **formal and informal organizations almost
    overnight**: clubs, churches, the school board, politics.
  - It overturned the stereotype of conformist, alienated suburbs. The real problems were little
    for teenagers to do, conflict, and difficulty with pluralism
    ([Gans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herbert_J._Gans)).
  - **For us:** Columbia's settlements are young. **Founders** fill civic roles early and hold them
    long, and the **teenager with nothing to do** is a stock role.
- **Jacobs and Duneier.** The **public character**, and **eyes on the street** (see 01). Street
  vendors, shopkeepers and porch-sitters are the town's watchers and messengers.
- **Oldenburg.** The **third place** and its **regulars**. The bartender, barber and café owner are
  hosts of the town's conversation.
- **Katz & Lazarsfeld.** **Opinion leaders** mediate between the news and everyone else.
- **di Leonardo.** **Kin-keepers** hold extended families together.
- **Small; Klinenberg.** The **institutions** of daily routine (childcare, church, library, gym,
  school) are where ties actually form, and the people who run them are brokers.
- **Putnam, *Bowling Alone* (2000).** The long decline of American associational life: fewer
  leagues, lodges and clubs, more solitary leisure. It is the reason civic roles today are held by
  a **small core of "joiners"** who do many of them.

## 2.3 What middle-American roles look like (the categories)
1. **Household and kin** (mostly ascribed): parent, spouse, child, teen, grandparent, sibling,
   caregiver, kin-keeper, breadwinner, homemaker, single, widowed, empty-nester, lodger.
2. **Work** (achieved), in the SOC major groups: care, retail, food, office, trades, transport,
   education, health, protective services, management and the professions.
3. **Civic and institutional** (achieved, often unpaid): council member, mayor, school board,
   PTA, church elder, coach, scout leader, volunteer firefighter, block or HOA president,
   festival organiser, union steward.
4. **Informal community** (emergent): public character, regular, opinion leader, broker,
   gatekeeper, gossip, busybody, helper, fixer, local historian, town character, town drunk,
   black sheep, newcomer, loner.
5. **Life stage and status**: student, retiree, unemployed, veteran, disabled, new arrival.
